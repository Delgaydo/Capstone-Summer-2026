### Chapter 1: Introduction

#### 1.1 Background of the Study
Mathematics achievement in high schools remains an important issue in educational policy, accountability systems, and school improvement efforts across the United States. In Florida, the Algebra 1 End-of-Course (EOC) exam plays a significant role because it is both a graduation requirement and a measure of student readiness for advanced mathematics courses. Previous studies have shown that factors such as socioeconomic status, ethnicity, school size, absenteeism, and family background can influence mathematics achievement. However, many traditional studies use ordinary least squares regression, which focuses only on average student performance and may overlook differences between low-performing and high-performing students.

Recent educational research has increasingly used Quantile Regression to examine how predictors affect different parts of the achievement distribution. Studies by Han and Whitacre (2016), Pani and Kishore (2016), Tian (2008), and Haile and Nguyen (2008) demonstrated that factors such as school size, absenteeism, ethnicity, and family background can have different effects depending on student performance levels. Despite this growing body of research, few studies have applied Quantile Regression to Florida mathematics achievement data, particularly Algebra 1 EOC outcomes.

This study addresses that gap by examining how demographic, socioeconomic, and school-level factors influence Algebra 1 EOC achievement across multiple achievement quantiles in Florida high schools. By using Quantile Regression, this research moves beyond average effects to better understand how these predictors impact struggling students differently from higher-achieving students. The findings may help educators, policymakers, and school leaders develop more targeted interventions and improve educational equity in mathematics achievement across Florida.

### Chapter 2: Literature Review

#### 2.1 Introduction
Mathematics achievement continues to be an important topic in education because of its connection to college readiness, career opportunities, and long-term academic success. In Florida, the Algebra 1 End-of-Course (EOC) exam is especially significant because it serves as both a graduation requirement and a measure of student readiness for advanced mathematics courses. Researchers have examined several factors that influence mathematics achievement, including demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, school-level conditions, attendance, and access to advanced coursework. Although many studies have explored mathematics performance, fewer studies have examined how these factors affect students differently across various achievement levels using Quantile Regression.

One important study related to Florida mathematics achievement was conducted by Hope D. Holley (2017). Holley examined predictive factors that affect achievement on Florida’s Algebra 1 EOC exam. The study focused on how socioeconomic status, ethnic diversity, district size, and standard-of-living indicators influenced student performance among first-time test takers in Florida public schools from 2010 to 2014. The findings showed that districts with higher percentages of socioeconomically disadvantaged and minority students had lower EOC passing rates. However, economically disadvantaged students performed better in districts with higher median household incomes. The study also found that district size, unemployment rates, and cost-of-living indexes were not significant predictors of achievement. In addition, Holley discussed concerns about high-stakes testing, including student labeling, reduced motivation, and the negative impact of standardized testing on deeper learning and problem-solving skills. While this study provides valuable insight into Florida EOC achievement, it used traditional statistical methods rather than Quantile Regression.

Another important study was conducted by Donna L. Edge (2009), who examined the relationship between Algebra I completion, demographic factors, and college readiness among high school students in a Texas suburban school district. The study investigated how variables such as gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and participation in the AVID program influenced advanced mathematics course completion and college readiness. Using statistical methods such as chi-square analysis, Cramer’s V, and multiple regression, the study found that students who completed Algebra I in eighth grade were more likely to complete advanced mathematics courses and demonstrate college readiness. The study also found significant relationships between college readiness and ethnicity, socioeconomic status, Algebra I completion, and AVID enrollment. Edge emphasized that early access to Algebra I can positively affect long-term academic outcomes, including college readiness and standardized test performance.

Several studies have also applied Quantile Regression to educational research. Han and Whitacre (2016) examined the relationship between school size and student performance in Oklahoma high schools using a two-stage spatial Quantile Regression model. Their findings showed that larger schools negatively affected student performance, especially among higher-performing students. Similarly, Pani and Kishore (2016) used Quantile Regression to study absenteeism and academic performance. Their results showed that absenteeism negatively affected all students, but low-performing students were impacted more severely than high-performing students. These findings demonstrate the usefulness of Quantile Regression because it allows researchers to examine how predictors affect students differently across the achievement distribution rather than only examining average performance.

Additional studies have explored the impact of family background and ethnicity on academic achievement using Quantile Regression. Tian (2008) found that family background factors influenced mathematical achievement differently across performance levels. Haile and Nguyen (2008) also found that ethnicity and family background had varying effects across quantiles of test score distributions. Their findings suggested that traditional mean-based regression models may overlook important differences between low-performing and high-performing students.

Although previous studies have examined mathematics achievement, demographic factors, and Quantile Regression separately, there appears to be a gap in the literature regarding Florida Algebra 1 EOC achievement. Specifically, none of the collected studies combined demographic, socioeconomic, and school-level predictors with Quantile Regression analysis using Florida high school mathematics achievement data. This study seeks to address that gap by applying Quantile Regression to analyze how demographic, socioeconomic, and school-level factors influence Algebra 1 EOC achievement across different student performance levels in Florida high schools. By examining differences across achievement quantiles, this research may provide a deeper understanding of educational inequality and help educators and policymakers develop more targeted intervention strategies for improving mathematics achievement.
